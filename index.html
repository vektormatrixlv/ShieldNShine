DETAILING STUDIO — JELGAVA

Cutting product cost per wash by switching to bulk concentrate

Independent single-bay detailing studio · ~40 vehicles/month

The problem

The studio was buying five separate retail-sized bottles — shampoo, foam, wheel cleaner, fabric cleaner, glass cleaner — reordering each on a different schedule, with no fixed dilution guidance beyond what was printed on each label.

The switch

Moving to two bulk concentrate drums (a general-purpose shampoo and a heavy-duty degreaser) with a single laminated dilution chart pinned above the mixing station removed the guesswork for whoever was on shift that day.

The result

Cost per wash dropped mainly from eliminating over-dilution — staff had been mixing roughly 30% stronger than needed "to be safe." A fixed ratio chart closed that gap without changing the wash process itself.

"We didn't change how we wash cars. We changed how much product each wash actually needed." — Illustrative studio owner quote, for demo purposes

The problem

Customer complaints clustered around inconsistency — a car washed on the morning shift looked noticeably better than the same service run on a weekend evening shift, despite using the same nominal product.

The switch

Rather than changing the wash equipment, the site introduced a single dilution chart per product and a short training session for each shift lead, so every bay was mixing to the same ratio regardless of who was working.

The result

Complaint volume tied to "patchy" results fell, without any hardware or process change — the variance had been coming from inconsistent mixing, not the wash cycle itself.

"The equipment was never the problem. The bottle-to-bucket ratio was different on every shift." — Illustrative site manager quote, for demo purposes

PAINT PROTECTION · GUIDE

How to wash a ceramic-coated car without ruining it

9 min read Updated for winter 2026 Sweden · Denmark · Ireland · Netherlands

If your car has a ceramic coating, the biggest threat to it isn't scratches — it's the wrong shampoo. A lot of everyday car soap is alkaline enough to strip a coating's hydrophobic layer within a handful of washes, which is exactly why picking a pH-neutral car shampoo matters more than any brush, mitt, or towel you'll buy. This guide walks through a wash method that gets salt and grime off without touching what you paid a detailer to put on.

We'll cover the pre-wash stage, the contact wash, drying, and the one habit most people get wrong in winter: washing too often with the wrong product, out of anxiety about road salt.

Why alkaline shampoo is the real enemy

Ceramic coatings are silica-based. Most consumer car shampoo is formulated to cut through grease fast, which usually means a higher pH — great for bare paint, but slowly corrosive to the coating's bonded layer. Over a winter of weekly washes, an alkaline shampoo can measurably shorten a coating's working life, even though nothing looks wrong on the surface.

A pH-neutral formula won't just protect the coating — it also won't strip any wax layered underneath it, which matters if you've topped the ceramic with a sacrificial wax before winter.

Step 1 — Pre-wash before anything touches the paint

Road salt carries sharp mineral particles. If you go straight in with a wash mitt, you're dragging that grit across the clear coat like sandpaper. A thick pre-wash foam left to dwell for a few minutes loosens the worst of it before contact.

What "coating-safe" actually means here

It means the foam is formulated to sit within a neutral pH band, so it won't start breaking down a ceramic layer even during a longer dwell time — which matters in freezing weather, when foam sits on the panel longer before you can rinse it off comfortably.

Step 2 — The contact wash

Once the loose grit is gone, this is where the pH-neutral shampoo does its work. Use the two-bucket method — one bucket of soap solution, one of clean rinse water — so you're not reloading your wash mitt with the same salt particles you just rinsed off it.

  • Work top to bottom, since the lower panels carry the most road grime.
  • Rinse the mitt in the clean bucket after every panel, not every few panels.
  • Avoid circular scrubbing motions — straight, overlapping passes reduce the chance of swirl marks.
Dull, hazy panel with visible mineral residue
Coating's hydrophobic sheeting restored

Step 3 — Drying without undoing the work

A rough towel introduces the swirl marks a careful wash just avoided. A clean, plush microfibre — used with light pressure, panel by panel — is worth the few extra minutes, especially in cold weather when water beads and freezes faster than you'd expect.

How often is too often?

Weekly is a reasonable cadence for a daily-driven car through a salted winter. Washing every second day "just in case" does more damage than the salt itself, because it multiplies the number of contact passes across the same panels. If you're worried about salt sitting on the car between washes, a quick rinse with plain water does most of the protective work without any chemical contact at all. This is exactly what the Wash Log tool is for — it tells you when you're actually due, instead of you guessing.

The takeaway

Coating-safe washing isn't about a special technique — it's about removing one variable: an alkaline shampoo doing slow damage you won't notice until the coating's performance quietly drops off months later. Get the pH right, keep the grit off the paint before contact, and the rest is just careful technique.
